## macOS Built-In Window Snapping

macOS has had [basic window tiling](https://support.apple.com/guide/mac-help/use-tiled-windows-on-your-mac-mchlef287e5d/mac) since macOS Sequoia (macOS 15). Before that, the only built-in option was the green full-screen button. Here is what macOS offers natively:

**Green button (hold):** Hover over the green button in the title bar. A menu appears with options to tile the window to the left or right half of the screen.

**Drag to edge (Sequoia only):** In macOS 15+, you can drag a window to the left or right edge of the screen to snap it to that half.

That is it. No thirds, no quarters, no keyboard shortcuts. For anything beyond basic left-right splitting, you need a third-party tool.

![Drag a window to snap it into place](/blog/snapback/snaps.webp)

## Why You Need Keyboard Shortcuts

Dragging windows to screen edges works, but it is slow. You have to grab the title bar, drag across the screen, wait for the snap indicator, and release. Doing this dozens of times a day adds up.

Keyboard shortcuts are faster. You press a key combination and the window snaps instantly. No dragging, no waiting, no imprecise positioning. Your hands stay on the keyboard.

## Drag to Snap

If you prefer mouse input for some actions, Snapback supports drag-to-snap:

![Drag to snap window positioning](/blog/snapback/drag-snap.webp)

1. Hold the Command key
2. Drag any window toward a screen edge or corner
3. A snap indicator appears showing where the window will land
4. Release to snap

This works for halves (edges), quarters (corners), and thirds (when dragging to the top or bottom edge).

Drag-to-snap is useful when you want to reposition a window quickly without remembering a shortcut. The keyboard shortcuts are faster for windows you snap to the same position repeatedly.

## Choosing Your Shortcuts

Here are some principles for picking shortcuts that stick:

**Use modifier combinations you do not already use.** If you use Command+Tab for app switching and Command+Space for Spotlight, avoid those. Common choices are Control+Option+Arrow keys or Command+Control+Arrow keys.

**Make related actions use related shortcuts.** If left half is Control+Option+Left, make right half Control+Option+Right. Consistency reduces cognitive load.

**Put your most-used actions on the easiest shortcuts.** If you snap to left half fifty times a day, that shortcut should be easy to hit without looking.

**Avoid conflicts with your IDE.** [VS Code](https://code.visualstudio.com/docs/getstarted/keybindings), IntelliJ, and other editors use many shortcuts. Check your editor's keymap before assigning Snapback shortcuts to avoid collisions.

## Setting Up Your First Shortcuts

1. Open Snapback from the menu bar
2. Go to Settings, then Hotkeys
3. Click on any action to assign a shortcut
4. Press your desired key combination
5. Test it by focusing a window and pressing the shortcut

If a shortcut conflicts with another app, Snapback will warn you. You can also check the [shortcuts help page](/apps/snapback/help#shortcuts) for more details on customization.

## Tips and Tricks

**Use shortcuts for your top three positions.** You probably snap most windows to the same two or three positions. Assign shortcuts to those and use drag-to-snap for everything else.

**Combine with workspace shortcuts.** Individual snapping is useful, but workspace shortcuts are more powerful. Save your ideal layout once, then restore it with one shortcut instead of snapping each window individually.

**Practice for a day.** The first day with new shortcuts feels awkward. By the second day, they become muscle memory. Give it 48 hours before deciding if a shortcut works for you.
